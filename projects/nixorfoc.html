NIXOR FOC

A 3-phase motor controller board for a Shell Eco-marathon car. The schematic is done, the board isn't yet.

ESP32 KiCad C++
Team Nixor Sharks is building a battery-electric prototype for Shell Eco-marathon Qatar, and this board is what turns a 48V pack into torque at the wheel. Three half-bridges, six MOSFETs, Hall-sensored commutation, and a hardware kill line that works whether or not the firmware is still running.

The Motor It's Built Around

  • 80BL D5BLD500-48A, 3-phase BLDC, 48V, 500W
  • 13A continuous, roughly 30A peak on acceleration, 2000rpm, 2.4N·m
  • Hall sensor feedback at 5V, level-shifted down to 3.3V before it ever reaches the ESP32
board layout, in progress

Power and the Kill Line

A 13S4P Li-ion pack, Samsung INR18650-35E cells, 48.1V nominal and 54.6V at full charge, behind a BMS that already handles balancing and cutoff. The board adds a main fuse on the positive lead, a pre-charge stage so closing the disconnect doesn't slam the bulk caps with inrush current, and a hardware KILL line that disables every gate driver directly, separate from whatever the firmware is doing. Two e-stops and the main disconnect all have to be able to cut traction, and every fault path defaults to drive off. Never the other way around.

The Line Worth Checking Twice

From the board's own requirements doc, written as a note to self before layout started: the gate driver's shutdown pin is tied straight to KILL. A normal IR2184 treats that pin as active-high shutdown, but the schematic draws it active-low. That is exactly the kind of one-bit mistake that would pass every visual check and only show up the first time somebody actually needed the kill line to work. Confirmed against the datasheet before it got anywhere near a populated board.

Last Year's Runaway

The throttle section of the firmware spec opens with why it exists: a runaway, the year before this one. The fix is boring on purpose. An out-of-range throttle reading commands zero and flags a fault instead of being treated as full throttle. Drive will not enable at power-on unless the throttle is already near zero. The command is rate-limited so a glitch cannot jump straight to full drive, and losing the signal entirely cuts to zero immediately. None of it is clever. All of it is the difference between a sensor glitch and a car that will not stop accelerating.

Thermal Is the Tight Part

Each of the six MOSFETs dissipates around 1.4W at 13A and 20kHz switching. Bare, in still air, the junction climbs to roughly 128°C, which is too hot. A heatsink rated around 5°C/W brings that down to about 49°C, checked against a 40°C Qatar ambient rather than a comfortable lab number. Six FETs needing heatsinking on a board this size is the actual layout constraint everything else has to fit around.
